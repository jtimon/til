Permanent umbrella issue for GUI music theory learning examples.

This issue is the durable record of the music discussion, terminology,
references, and plans. Code comments explain implementation. Keep this

| Chord on Do | Notes       | 12-TET offsets | Symbol |
|-------------+-------------+----------------+--------|
| Major       | Do Mi Sol   | 0 4 7          | C      |
| Minor       | Do Mib Sol  | 0 3 7          | Cm     |
| Diminished  | Do Mib Solb | 0 3 6          | Cdim   |
| Augmented   | Do Mi Sol#  | 0 4 8          | Caug   |

Do-Mi-Sol simultaneously is a C-major triad; successively it is an arpeggio.
Do-Mi-Sol-Si is Cmaj7, Do-Mi-Sol-Sib is C7 (dominant seventh), and
Do-Mib-Sol-Sib is Cm7. Begin with triads before adding these variations.

Count distinct pitches modulo the octave, not voices: Do-Mi-Sol-Do is still
a triad. In lead-sheet notation C6 adds La (1-3-5-6); in figured bass a 6
can instead mean a triad in first inversion (e.g. Mi-Sol-Do, C/E).
For Nathex transfer the degree rule, then check the actual intervals. In
a five-degree mode, degree 6 is the root one hextave up. Adding it to a
Nathex triad doubles the root at the octave rather than adding a fourth
pitch class. Degree 7, on the other hand, is degree 2 in the next register.
Sources: [[https://musictheory.pugetsound.edu/mt21c/Triads.html][Hutchinson, Triads]];
[[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sixth_chord][Sixth chord (added sixth vs first inversion)]].

*** Tuning FAQ: notation, equal temperament, harmonics, and Bach

**** Names and formulas

- N-TET means N-tone equal temperament; here it divides 2:1 into N equal
  frequency-ratio steps. N-EDO (equal divisions of the octave) is explicit.
  ~f(k) = f0 * 2^(k/N)~; one step is ~1200/N~ cents.
- Nathex's actual tuning is simply 8-TET/8-EDO. The user prefers established
  terminology such as 8-TET to the invented name. The ca/de/fi/go/lu notation
  and mode choices are a separate layer, not a reason to require that name.
  UI/code renaming is a follow-up; existing identifiers still say Nathex.
- Regular Global hextaves retain 12-TET: names repeat every eight semitones,
  an 800-cent span with ratio ~2^(8/12)~. Three such cycles equal two octaves.
  This is not 8-TET. Its selected modes reorganize pitches as well as names.
- Nathex uses eight 150-cent steps per 1200-cent doubling. One Nathex hextave
  is one physical octave. Do not mix its register count with regular hextaves.
- Both share five natural names at offsets ~0,2,3,5,7~ of an eight-step cycle;
  Europe uses steps ~2,1,2,2,1~. A five-note mode is a subset of the grid.
- Halving 8-TET steps gives 16-TET (75 cents), then 32-TET (37.5 cents).
  Halving 12-TET steps gives 24-TET (50 cents), then 48-TET (25 cents).
  Quarter-tone 24-TET is one common microtonal system, not all microtonality.
- Fitting 2/3/4/5 eight-step cycles into *two* octaves yields respectively
  8/12/16/20-TET. With each step halved, these become 16/24/32/40-TET.
  This changes the tuning; merely displaying more cycles does not improve it.

**** What is natural? Why Hz and cents tell different stories

- Possible pitches are continuous (the useful analog/digital analogy).
  A harmonic series is discrete: ~f0, 2*f0, 3*f0, ...~. It is equally spaced
  in Hz, not in musical intervals. Successive ratios ~2/1,3/2,4/3,...~ shrink.
- There is no single natural scale. Just intonation selects simple ratios
  between pitches; different roots and harmonic contexts favor different
  relationships. Bells and other inharmonic sounds need not have integer
  partials. Timbre affects which intervals sound smooth.
- An octave is 2:1. Cents measure ratios: ~1200*log2(f2/f1)~. TET grids have
  equal cents spacing but increasing Hz spacing higher up a linear-Hz plot.
- From a 100-Hz fundamental, two octaves (100..400 Hz) contain harmonics 1..4.
  From 400..1600 Hz of that *same* fundamental they contain harmonics 4..16,
  thirteen pitches including endpoints. Harmonic 16 is four octaves above
  harmonic 1; it does not mean sixteen subdivisions per octave.
- Two octaves of 12-TET are 24 steps, or 25 pitches including both endpoints.
- Error in cents for a given target ratio stays the same in every register
  and from every grid note in an equal temperament. Error in Hz grows with
  frequency. This holds beyond C1..B9 mathematically, not as a claim that all
  such frequencies are audible or physically playable.
- Unequal temperament can have different errors from different starting
  notes. Rounded integer-Hz playback tables introduce extra error, especially
  low down; comparison charts should calculate at higher precision.

**** What Bach did and did not do

- Octaves, sharps/flats, and ratio-based tuning predate Bach by centuries.
  Bach did not invent 12-TET or the octave. His Well-Tempered Clavier explores
  all 24 major/minor keys with a tuning usable throughout them.
- Earlier practice was not one formula everyone used. Pythagorean tuning
  builds from fifths (3:2); just intonation includes ratios such as 5:4;
  meantone and other temperaments adjust intervals to suit fixed instruments.
- The main conflict is changing key, not changing octave. Twelve pure fifths
  do not equal seven octaves: ~(3/2)^12 / 2^7 = 1.01364...~, 23.46 cents.
- Well temperament often means unequal but usable keys, with different key
  colors. Modern equal temperament makes transposed interval patterns identical.
  Bach's exact intended tuning is disputed. Do not label 12-TET 'Bach's scale'.
  The linked Spanish article blurs this distinction in places.
- In ideal strings, halving vibrating length doubles frequency at fixed
  tension/mass per length. String-length ratios and frequency ratios express
  the same physics inversely; they are not incompatible tuning standards.
- Sharps/flats specify alterations, not automatic harmonic corrections.
  C# and Db coincide in 12-TET, but need not in other tuning systems.

Reproduce each entry with ~c = 1200*log2(ratio)~ and
~abs(round(c*N/1200)*1200/N - c)~. The table concerns best available single
intervals, not proof that all such choices form mutually pure chords.

- 8 -> 16 -> 32 and 12 -> 24 -> 48 retain every old grid pitch: nearest
  approximation never worsens. Unrelated grids do not provide that guarantee.
- A pure fifth is 701.96 cents: 12/24 offer 700, 16 offers 675, 32 offers
  712.5. 32 improves on 16 but does not beat 12/24 for this ratio.
- Among the first twelve rows, 32 beats 24 five times, loses three, ties four.
  But distinct octave-reduced harmonics 1..16 give targets
  ~3/2,5/4,7/4,9/8,11/8,13/8,15/8~: 24 wins four and 32 wins three.
  Counting wins depends on targets, weighting, and duplicated complementary
  intervals (fourth/fifth). There is no universal best musical system.
- Worst-case nearest-pitch error is ~600/N~ cents: 50 for 12, 37.5 for 16,
  25 for 24, 18.75 for 32. A finer unrelated grid improves this bound, not
  necessarily every chosen harmonic match. 24 and 32 share ticks every 150 cents.
- Moving sharp labels changes notation, not sound. Moving actual pitches can
  improve selected ratios, but ceases to be strict TET. For example, move a
  16-TET third from 375 to 386.31 cents or a fifth from 675 to 701.96.
  Improving one root/context changes intervals elsewhere. A finite fixed
  keyboard cannot make every fifth and third pure in every key.
- Keeping the same original eight pitches after adding micro-frets does not
  change their errors: new choices must actually be used in melodies/chords.
- Fretless strings (violin, etc.) can adapt to different systems without new
  frets, at the cost of ear/position training. Frets supply repeatable targets;
  bends provide some flexibility. Hole count on a flute is not pitch count:
  cross-fingering, partial covering, and registers matter.

*** Harmonic series listening view and longer-term tuning comparison

The Tuning comparison tab plays exact partials (n*f0) or their nearest
C4-anchored 12-, 8-, or 16-TET pitches. Click either frequency in a row or
listen to its column in sequence. Keep one beat per harmonic even when the
approximation repeats a pitch (13 and 14 in the C2/8-TET comparison).
Positive cents mean the grid is sharp.
The display starts at C4: choose f0=C2 for partials 4..16 over two octaves,
or f0=C4 for partials 1..4. Choose one regular hextave (800 cents), one
octave (1200), two regular hextaves (1600), or two octaves (2400).
The Delian constant D = cube root of 2 is computed at compile time. From
the same starting pitch, a regular hextave spans 2/D, a Nathex hextave 2:
their endpoint ratio is D. Both system names are retained.

The separate Harmonics and just scale staff has partial ranges 1..8, 1..16
and 1..32, starting on B2 by default (C4 is also available). Partial 32
is five octaves above the fundamental. The spacing in Hz is constant;
the step from n to n+1 in cents is 1200*log2((n+1)/n), so it shrinks.
This follows from frequency ratios, not from volume or the Delian constant.
Dyads skip one partial: (1,3), (3,5), (5,7), etc. Extend the same stack
to triads or tetrads. Clicking another starting partial auditions that stack
if all its voices are in range. A dyad is any pair of pitches; this is one
particular way to select them. Greek, Global, Nathex and just Ionian also
offer degree 1+3 dyads. Use PT/ES/EN Tetrade/Tetrada/Tetrad for four notes.
The series has no Ionian/Dorian modes.
For comparison, its C major (Ionian) control plays one five-limit just
intonation: 1, 9/8, 5/4, 4/3, 3/2, 5/3, 15/8, 2. It repeats at double
frequency for the second octave (15 notes including the shared boundary).
This is one diatonic tuning, not Bach's uniquely intended original scale.

The separate 12-TET circle starts on C. Click any pitch class, or play
an ascending fifth (+7 semitones) clockwise or fourth (+5) anticlockwise.
Each move sounds the starting note followed by the interval; after twelve
steps either direction returns to the starting pitch class.

Separate listening idea: make D itself the frequency ratio of one whole
eight-step hextave. Then one step is D^(1/8) = 2^(1/24), or 50 cents;
three hextaves span one octave. The available pitches are 24-TET, but
ca/de/fi/go/lu would repeat every 400 cents. This is an experimental
alternative to Nathex's 2:1 cycle, not a change to its tuning.

Listening observation (one listener): individual Nathex chords could sound
good while the sequence felt unfamiliar, especially immediately after 12-TET.
Listening longer within one tuning changed the impression. This motivates
switching approximations while holding the reference and register fixed.

**** Listening experiment: one MIDI passage, several translations

Use a familiar passage from a standard MIDI file as the 12-TET baseline.
Keep timing, voices, dynamics, timbre, and a reference pitch fixed so the
listener can attribute differences to pitch mapping. Try the original,
Nathex (8-TET), and its 16-TET subdivision. Compare these separate rules:

- Keep signed step counts from an anchor but change the step size. Keep the
  user's "Delian constant trick" as a candidate; record its exact mapping
  before identifying it with this rule. A constant frequency multiplier
  alone transposes all voices without changing their interval ratios.
- Keep the register and round each source pitch to the nearest target-grid
  pitch. For a semitone offset s from the anchor, use round(s*N/12) steps
  of N-TET. Octaves stay octaves; on a coarser grid two notes may coincide.
- Choose ratios for an intended chord (e.g. 4:5:6 for a major triad), then
  approximate them on the target grid. Consider voice leading and held
  notes: choosing a new root every chord can move a sustained voice.

MIDI interoperability matters for sharing with other programs as well as
importing songs. Plain note numbers under default tuning cannot reproduce
every 8/16-TET pitch. Test export with a receiving synth that supports a
tuning table or independent bends for simultaneous voices. Future experiment;
no file format, playback mode or MIDI implementation decided here.

*** Proposed interactive exercises: listen, explore, then Check

Build a chord from a chosen root. Play any key to set the root; the prompt
names a quality, e.g. "Build Dm7 on Re." Listen plays the target as often as
needed without revealing its keys on the staff, keyboard, or piano roll.
Try candidate chords on the keyboard; deliberately wrong notes are part of
practice. Only Check submits the currently chosen chord. No automatic success,
red keys, penalties, or attempts counted while exploring or replaying the clue.

On Check, answer yes/no. Accept Re-Fa-La-Do in any order, octave, or doubling
for Dm7 unless the prompt explicitly asks for an inversion or register.
Re-Fa#-La-Do is a useful near miss: offer "Listen to the third" after checking,
then let the learner retry the same root. Use PT/ES/EN names and symbols.

Other exercises can use the same Listen / explore / Check interaction:
- Change one voice: start with C major and make it minor while keeping Do and
  Sol. On Check compare the two thirds, then repeat from a different root.
- Find the inversion: hear a triad and put its third in the bass. Here Check
  tests the lowest sounding pitch as well as the three pitch classes.
- Match a harmonic: play partial 7, then ask for its octave partner among
  partials 8..16. Let the learner audition freely; on Check, 14 is correct
  because 14*f0 is twice 7*f0, regardless of its approximate staff spelling.
- Hear a tuning difference: alternate a pure 3:2 fifth with its nearest
  8-TET fifth from the same bass. Ask which upper pitch is sharper; replay
  both on demand. After Check show 750 vs 701.96 cents. Ask which the learner
  prefers separately: preference is not a graded answer.

*** Next piano input: play a real MIDI keyboard

Receive live MIDI Note On/Off alongside the existing QWERTY and mouse
inputs in ~examples/piano.til~. MIDI is the message protocol: a class-
compliant USB-MIDI keyboard and a five-pin DIN keyboard connected through
a MIDI interface can feed the same input port abstraction. No MIDI file
or audio capture is needed. Start with MIDI notes 48..83 (C3..B5); middle
C (60) should light C4, or ca4 in Nathex. Use velocity, handle Note On
with zero velocity as release, and verify overlapping notes in the piano
roll, including release/disconnection without stuck notes.

*** Later instrument design: virtual flute before printing

- Explore target scale, range, fingering, hole count, spacing, bore, hole sizes,
  and wall/chimney dimensions virtually before designing a printable flute.
- ~L_eff = c/(2*f)~ is only an open-pipe starting approximation. Tone holes are
  acoustic vents with corrections and interactions, not exact new endpoints.
  Air temperature, blowing, and end corrections affect physical tuning.
- A six-hole D fipple/whistle was a suggestion, not a user-selected design.
  Printable format/tooling and physical layout remain to be decided explicitly.
- Measure and tune physical prototypes; label predicted results as estimates.

*** Eventual extraction into dawtil

The music application and its learning material will eventually have their
own repository, named ~dawtil~ (a play on Spanish /datil/, the fruit of
the date palm). The precedent is tiled, which grew out of the editor example
and took tilminal with it. nostril is another til project with its own repo.

- Before extraction, consider retaining reusable music functionality as a
  library in til's ~src/std/~. Candidate pieces include frequency/ratio/cents
  conversion, equal-temperament calculations, scale and chord construction,
  and reusable synthesis primitives. These are candidates for review, not
  approved new modules or public APIs.
- Also explore an audio-only ~mode music~ for small programs that play notes,
  chords, or sequences without opening a GUI. It could provide playback
  setup, scheduling, waiting for playback to finish, and cleanup, backed by
  the same reusable library. Compare this convenience with ordinary library
  imports before deciding whether a separate mode is worthwhile. The name,
  syntax, backend dependencies, and mode contract remain design questions;
  this records the idea rather than introducing a mode now.
- Separate musical calculations from GUI rendering and audio-device/backend
  handling. Project-specific modes, notation experiments, songs, controls,
  and teaching views can live in the music repository, consuming any agreed
  standard-library functionality instead of copying it.
- Decide the standard-library boundary, names, contracts, tests, and install
  behavior with the user before moving code. The standalone build should
  exercise any retained library through the installed til distribution.
- Inventory the music-specific pieces together: ~examples/music_modes.til~,
  ~examples/piano.til~, ~examples/guitar.til~, ~examples/lib/music.til~,
  chord/audio helpers, assets, tests, and the teaching material recorded here.
  Include future tuning-comparison and instrument-design work in that review.
- Identify which dependencies belong to til's general-purpose GUI/audio and
  standard libraries, versus application-specific code that should travel
  with the music project. Review the tiled extraction for practical lessons
  before deciding the exact boundary; avoid copying compiler/runtime internals.
- Establish a reproducible build and test path using an identified til
  version from outside the compiler checkout. Verify asset lookup, GUI/audio
  dependencies, and supported platforms without relying on til's local
  ~bin/~, ~tmp/~, or source-tree-relative imports.
- Choose repository ownership, layout, and migration timing with
  the user. Transfer the relevant documentation, references, tests, and open
  plans along with the application so this discussion is not lost.
- Make the new repository the single maintained home for the music
  application. At migration time, decide which examples, if any, are worth
  keeping in the language repo for teaching or compiler regression coverage;
  do not maintain two diverging application
  copies. Leave clear pointers from til to the extracted project.
- Validate the standalone project before removing or replacing anything in
  til. Track migration progress here until its music roadmap has an agreed
  home in the new repository.

*** References and lower-priority alternative systems

Priority is standard harmony and the 12/8/16/24/32-TET experiments above.

- [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Well-Tempered_Clavier][Bach: intended tuning and its uncertainty]]
- [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well_temperament][Well temperament]]
- [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equal_temperament][Equal temperament]]
- [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohlen%E2%80%93Pierce_scale][Bohlen-Pierce scale]]:
  common equal version is 13 equal divisions of a 3:1 tritave (13-ED3), not
  thirteen divisions of an octave. Step ~146.30 cents, close to 8-TET's 150,
  but eight BP steps are ~1170.43 cents and thirteen are exactly 1901.96.
  Thirteen 8-TET steps are 1950 cents. BP has odd-harmonic motivations such
  as 3:5:7 chords, just variants, and nine-note subsets of its thirteen-note
  grid. Do not confuse the subset size with the equal grid's step count.
